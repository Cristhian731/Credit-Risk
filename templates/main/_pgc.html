1. Project title

Credit Risk and Loan Viability Prediction Platform: a machine learning web system that supports the lending decision of a credit analyst. Universidad de Cundinamarca, Machine Learning (seventh semester). Team: Cristhian Stiven Aza Diaz, Harrison Camargo and Alejandro Alarcon.

2. Problem statement and justification

The problem

A lender must decide whether to grant a loan to a person whose future behavior it cannot observe. If it approves a loan that is not repaid, it loses money; if it rejects one that would have been repaid, it loses a customer and denies credit to someone who deserved it. Manual evaluation is slow, differs from one analyst to another and leaves little record of why a decision was taken.

Context

Credit risk is one of the main sources of vulnerability of banks. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (2017) requires institutions to hold capital in proportion to their credit exposure, because wrong lending decisions produce non-performing loans and provisioning costs. Traditional scorecards capture the non-linear relations among the attributes of a borrower less well than machine learning classifiers (Lessmann et al., 2015; Dastile et al., 2020).

Affected population

Three groups are affected: applicants, who wait for an answer and need it to be fair and explained; credit analysts, who review many requests with little time; and the institution, which carries the losses of its defaults and answers to the regulator. Young applicants are a special case: in the data of this project, applicants aged 18 to 24 default more often than older ones (21% against 5% at ages 55 to 69), so a model that uses age can treat them very differently from the rest.

Evidence

In Colombia, the past-due loan quality indicator (ICM, the past-due portfolio divided by the gross portfolio) fell to 3.8% in May 2026, its lowest level since December 2022, but it was still 5% for consumer loans, the highest of all categories (El Colombiano, 2026, with data from the Superintendencia Financiera de Colombia). The Banco de la República (2026) warned that households increased their debt-to-income ratios and that credit risk showed an incipient deterioration in segments such as consumer credit, and the Superintendencia Financiera de Colombia (2026) reports that the past-due portfolio decreased in 2025 but remains a variable that institutions must provision for and monitor. This local evidence was compiled by Harrison Camargo (docs/pgc/problem_evidence.md). The public dataset used (Loan Default Prediction Dataset, 255,347 loans, 18 columns) shows a default rate of 11.6%, so about one loan in nine is not repaid and a model that approves everything would accept all of those losses. The evaluation of the three candidate models confirmed that the signal is limited: the champion reaches a mean cross-validation ROC AUC of 0.711 and a recall of 0.664 for the defaulters, with a precision of about 0.20 (Methodology, phase 4). Most flagged applicants would have repaid, which is why the system never decides alone.

Consequences

Without a consistent estimate, the institution approves risky loans or rejects good ones. With a model used without control, the opposite problem appears: the evaluation by age group showed that the champion flags 85% of the applicants aged 18 to 24 and only 6% of those aged 55 to 69 (phase 5). Using that output as an automatic decision would be unfair and hard to defend.

Relevance and how the system helps

The platform estimates the risk of each request in the server with the champion model, stores every prediction with its date, user, result and model version, and sends the request to an analyst, who approves, rejects or asks for documents and always writes a reason. A human review is mandatory for High Risk results and for applicants aged 18 to 24. The system records whether the analyst followed or contradicted the model, shows indicators and monitors errors, response times and, when real outcomes exist, the actual AUC and recall (Bussmann et al., 2021). The model assists; the person decides.

3. General objective

Develop and deploy a web platform that estimates the viability of a loan from the financial information of an applicant, using a machine learning model selected with documented criteria, to support the decision of a credit analyst with a traceable, role-based process.

5. Reference framework

Background and related studies

Lessmann et al. (2015) compared 41 classifiers on several credit datasets and found that ensembles outperform individual classifiers and the industry-standard logistic regression. Dastile et al. (2020) reviewed credit scoring models and described a move toward ensembles and deep learning, with the lack of interpretability as the main weakness. Gunnarsson et al. (2021) showed that deep networks give only marginal gains over tuned gradient boosting on tabular credit data, so simpler models are preferable in practice. Bussmann et al. (2021) showed that explainable techniques make credit models understandable for risk managers and regulators.

Theoretical foundations and technical concepts

  • Binary classification. The task assigns each loan to repaid (0) or defaulted (1) and returns a probability. The positive class is the default.
  • Logistic regression, random forest and XGBoost. A linear baseline, a bagged ensemble of trees and a boosted ensemble of trees. XGBoost was introduced by Chen and Guestrin (2016).
  • ROC AUC and recall. The ROC AUC is the probability that the model gives a higher risk to a defaulter than to a borrower who repaid; recall is the share of real defaulters that the model detects.
  • Cross-validation and the test split. Models are compared on the train split only; the test split is evaluated once to report, never to choose.
  • Dimensionality reduction (PCA). PCA keeps the directions of largest variance (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016). It is not necessarily the best for prediction.
  • Clustering. K-Means, DBSCAN and agglomerative clustering group applicants without using the label; the silhouette (Rousseeuw, 1987) measures how well separated the groups are.
  • CRISP-ML(Q). A process model for machine learning with quality assurance, from business understanding to monitoring and maintenance (Studer et al., 2021).

Relation with the project

The studies support the design: a logistic regression baseline compared with two ensembles, results published in read-only pages, explainability limited to what the model really shows, and a human analyst who justifies each decision. The experiments of the project agree with the literature: the three models are within 0.009 of ROC AUC of each other on cross-validation, a small difference that is typical of tabular credit data. The champion was not the model with the highest AUC but the simplest one among the tied models, according to the rule defined before training.

6. Methodology and development schedule

The six CRISP-ML(Q) phases as applied

  1. Business and data understanding. Problem, stakeholders, dataset (Kaggle, CC0), success criteria fixed before training (CV ROC AUC ≥ 0.70, recall ≥ 0.60, gap with the test split ≤ 0.02). No domain expert was consulted; this is declared as a limitation.
  2. Data engineering. Validation, cleaning, 80/20 stratified split, features of the champion pipeline, and exploration with PCA and t-SNE. With nearly uncorrelated variables, PCA found nothing to compress and reducing to two components dropped the CV ROC AUC from 0.711 to 0.532.
  3. Model engineering. Three models, randomized search, 5-fold cross-validation, the champion rule (highest mean ROC AUC; models less than 0.01 apart are tied and the highest recall wins) and an unsupervised clustering analysis. K-Means, DBSCAN and agglomerative clustering found the same four groups, which only reproduce the employment types and separate the real default rate weakly.
  4. Model evaluation. Test metrics reported once; no overfitting beyond the threshold; the champion is the logistic regression (test ROC AUC 0.718, recall 0.674), although XGBoost has the highest AUC.
  5. Deployment. The champion is served by a Flask application on Render with PostgreSQL. The age finding was handled with a mandatory human review for applicants aged 18 to 24 and for High Risk results, and the model without age was tested and rejected (CV ROC AUC 0.652).
  6. Monitoring and maintenance. Logs of errors and response times, population stability (PSI) against the training distribution, real AUC and recall when outcomes exist, versioned artifacts and a retraining rule.

Development and integration of the web system

The system was built in iterations (Scrum), with one branch and one reviewed pull request per block, automated tests and continuous integration on SQLite and PostgreSQL. Training is offline (python -m ml.train) and writes versioned artifacts; the web application only reads them. Both sides share three contracts: the feature adapter, metrics.json and manifest.json. The server applies the same preprocessing as the training and scores every request, so the browser never holds the model. The request flow is: the applicant creates a request, pre-filled from their finances; the champion scores it; the analyst reviews it (mandatory for High Risk or ages 18 to 24) and approves, rejects or requests documents with a reason; the applicant sees the status; later the real outcome (repaid or defaulted) is recorded.

Results and decisions of the finished project

  • Champion by tie rule. Logistic regression (mean CV ROC AUC 0.711, recall 0.664). XGBoost (0.720) and random forest (0.716) are within 0.01 (0.009 and 0.005) of the best, so the rule picks the simplest with higher recall; the success criteria were all met.
  • Age and human review. The model flags 85% of the applicants aged 18 to 24. Removing age lowered the ROC AUC to 0.652, so age stays and human review is mandatory for that group.
  • No domain expert. No expert reviewed the work; the conclusions about business value are therefore expected, not demonstrated.
  • PCA and clustering. Neither improves the model: PCA discards signal, and the clusters are the employment types. They are published as evidence, not used in production.
  • Operations. Every inference is logged with its time and model version; monitoring and indicators are in the private area (administrator and analyst).
