How it works

A random forest trains many decision trees. Each tree sees a random sample of the applicants and, at every split, a random subset of the features. The forest averages the probabilities of all its trees (Breiman, 2001): one tree is unstable, but the average of many different trees is much steadier.

Why it fits this problem

Trees work on tabular data without scaling and capture non-linear relations and interactions, such as an age effect that depends on the employment type. Balanced class weights compensate for the few defaults.

Strengths and limits

It is robust to outliers and rarely overfits badly. It is the largest and slowest of the three models, and harder to explain than a linear model: the feature importance shows what matters, not how. Its size was limited with shallow trees and large leaves so that the saved model stays small.

Hyperparameters in this tab

  • n_estimators: number of trees; more trees give a steadier average at a higher cost. Selected: {{ p.get('n_estimators', 'n/a') }}.
  • max_depth: maximum depth of each tree. Selected: {{ p.get('max_depth', 'n/a') }}.
  • min_samples_leaf: minimum applicants in a leaf; larger leaves smooth the predictions. Selected: {{ p.get('min_samples_leaf', 'n/a') }}.
  • max_features: features tried at each split (sqrt is the square root of their number). Selected: {{ p.get('max_features', 'n/a') }}.
  • class_weight: weight of each class. Selected: {{ m.params.get('class_weight', 'n/a') }}.
