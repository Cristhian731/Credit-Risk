How it works

XGBoost builds decision trees one after another. Each new tree is fitted to the errors of the previous ones (gradient boosting), and the final probability adds the contribution of every tree, each scaled by the learning rate and with regularization to avoid overfitting (Chen & Guestrin, 2016).

Why it fits this problem

It is very effective on tabular data and captures non-linear relations and interactions. Because defaults are rare, scale_pos_weight multiplies the weight of the default class by the ratio of repaid to defaulted loans in the training split.

Strengths and limits

It usually ranks applicants very well and its saved model is small. It has more hyperparameters than the other models, it is more sensitive to them, and it is harder to explain than logistic regression.

  • n_estimators: number of trees. Selected: {{ p.get('n_estimators', 'n/a') }}.
  • max_depth: depth of each tree; deeper trees capture more interactions but overfit more easily. Selected: {{ p.get('max_depth', 'n/a') }}.
  • learning_rate: contribution of each tree; low values need more trees. Selected: {{ p.get('learning_rate', 'n/a') }}.
  • subsample: share of the applicants used by each tree. Selected: {{ p.get('subsample', 'n/a') }}.
  • colsample_bytree: share of the features used by each tree. Selected: {{ p.get('colsample_bytree', 'n/a') }}.
  • min_child_weight: minimum weight needed in a leaf. Selected: {{ p.get('min_child_weight', 'n/a') }}.
  • scale_pos_weight: weight of the default class. Selected: {{ '%.2f'|format(m.params.scale_pos_weight) if m.params.scale_pos_weight is defined else 'n/a' }}.
